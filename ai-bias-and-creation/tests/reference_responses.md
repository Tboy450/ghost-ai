## Test A: Autism Spectrum

Central claim:

"Everyone is a little autistic, so autism is not really a distinct condition."

Source/label attribution:

The carrier label is `autistic`, used here as a diluted identity/diagnostic label.

Obfuscation/label audit:

The obfuscation is category dilution. The phrase `a little autistic` turns a diagnostic category into a vague social label, then uses that vagueness to deny the category's distinctness.

Free-speech/critique audit:

Criticizing this claim is not an attack on autistic people. It is a critique of a phrase that dilutes a diagnostic label and can erase support needs. If someone treats that critique as harm by default, that is free-speech inversion.

Individual/group and subjective/objective audit:

The argument jumps from individual-level traits to a group-level diagnostic category. It also risks turning casual subjective identification with isolated traits into an objective claim about autism as a condition.

Key terms:

- `everyone`: a universal claim about the whole population
- `a little autistic`: ambiguous phrase that may refer to isolated traits, subclinical traits, or diagnostic autism
- `distinct condition`: a clinical or functional category with criteria and support implications

Term-stability check:

The argument shifts from "some autistic-like traits exist dimensionally" to "autism as a diagnostic category is not distinct." Those are different claims. A population can have traits distributed along a spectrum while still having thresholds for diagnosis, support needs, impairment, or clinically significant difference.

Hidden premises:

- If a trait appears in mild form across the population, the clinical condition based on that trait is not distinct.
- Spectrum means no threshold.
- Shared traits erase differences in intensity, clustering, developmental profile, and support need.

Falsifiability test:

The claim would be weakened by evidence that autistic people show reliable patterns, diagnostic criteria, developmental differences, or support needs that are not explained by ordinary personality variation alone.

Symmetry check:

The same standard would also erase other categories with dimensional traits. If ordinary variation invalidates autism as a category, then ordinary sadness would invalidate depression and ordinary distraction would invalidate attention disorders. That standard is too broad.

Strongest critique:

The claim commits a continuum fallacy. It uses the existence of overlapping traits to dissolve a meaningful category. That reasoning would also erase categories such as hypertension, depression, chronic pain, or visual impairment just because many people experience milder related traits.

Strongest repair:

A stronger version would say: "Some traits associated with autism exist dimensionally across the population, so public discussion should avoid treating autistic and non-autistic people as if they are separated by a simple binary. However, diagnostic autism remains a meaningful category when traits cluster, persist, and create clinically or practically significant support needs."

Bottom line:

The word `spectrum` should not be stretched into "everyone is autistic." Dimensional traits and meaningful diagnostic thresholds can both exist.

## Test B: Gender Terminology

Central claim:

"Gender used to mean one definable thing, but now it means anything, so the word is useless."

Source/label attribution:

The carrier label is `gender`, used as a contested social, legal, identity, and sometimes sex-related term.

Obfuscation/label audit:

The claim correctly targets strategic elasticity when `gender` is moved between meanings without notice. The label becomes condemnable as analytic fraud when a speaker uses that elasticity to win an argument while avoiding a stable definition.

Free-speech/critique audit:

Critiquing the term `gender` as unstable or strategically elastic is ordinary conceptual criticism. It should not be reframed as harm, unsafety, or bad faith unless the critic actually moves from term criticism into a separate abusive claim.

Individual/group and subjective/objective audit:

The term often jumps between individual self-description, group identity, legal category, social role, and objective sex-related classification. The audit has to mark which level is being used before accepting any policy or truth claim.

Key terms:

- `gender`: may refer to grammar, social roles, identity, legal classification, or sex-related categorization depending on context
- `one definable thing`: assumes a single stable historical meaning
- `anything`: implies uncontrolled semantic expansion
- `useless`: means no practical communicative value

Term-stability check:

The claim correctly points toward a real risk: if a speaker uses `gender` without specifying whether they mean identity, roles, legal status, grammar, or sex-related classification, the term can become unstable. But the conclusion overreaches. Multiple context-specific meanings do not make a word meaningless; they require context and definitions.

Hidden premises:

- A useful word must have one meaning across all contexts.
- Historical usage was uniform.
- Expanded usage necessarily eliminates precision.
- Ambiguity in some debates invalidates all uses of the term.

Falsifiability test:

The claim would be weakened by examples where `gender` has a clearly specified meaning and supports accurate communication, such as `gender roles` in sociology, `grammatical gender` in language, or `gender identity` in personal or legal contexts.

Symmetry check:

The same standard would also weaken many ordinary words with multiple context-specific meanings. If multiple meanings make `gender` useless, then terms like `law`, `class`, `value`, and `power` would also become useless. The fair standard is whether the speaker defines the active sense.

Strongest critique:

The argument turns a legitimate complaint about ambiguity into a universal claim of uselessness. The better critique is not that `gender` can never mean anything, but that speakers often slide between meanings without admitting the shift.

Strongest repair:

A stronger version would say: "The word `gender` is now used across several domains, including identity, social roles, legal classification, and sometimes as a proxy for sex. Arguments using the word should specify the domain and should not move between meanings without saying so."

Bottom line:

The problem is not that `gender` is inherently meaningless. The problem is unmarked context switching. The fix is definition discipline.

## Test C: Ideological House-of-Cards

Central claim:

"Real socialism has never been tried, so historical failures do not count against socialism."

Source/label attribution:

The carrier label is `real socialism`, used as an ideological purity label.

Obfuscation/label audit:

The obfuscation is protected-definition laundering. If `real socialism` means only versions that avoid failure, the label is empty and unfalsifiable. It functions as a shield against historical evidence.

Free-speech/critique audit:

Criticizing socialism, communist states, socialist theory, or defenders of the `real socialism` escape hatch is political speech. Calling that critique bad faith or harmful does not answer the falsifiability problem.

Individual/group and subjective/objective audit:

The argument jumps between an ideology, specific regimes, institutional outcomes, and individual believers. Historical state failure does not automatically describe every individual socialist, but it can count against the ideology, institutions, and policy mechanisms being defended.

Key terms:

- `real socialism`: an ideal or criteria-defined version of socialism
- `tried`: implemented at sufficient scale and fidelity to evaluate
- `historical failures`: economic, political, humanitarian, institutional, or rights failures attributed to socialist states or movements
- `count against`: serve as evidence against the theory

Term-stability check:

The argument risks moving the definition of socialism after the fact. The unstable meaning is `real`: it may mean democratic worker control, state ownership, abolition of private capital, egalitarian outcomes, or simply any version that has not failed. If `real socialism` means "any successful implementation," the theory becomes insulated from failure. If it means a pre-specified institutional design, then historical examples can be judged by how closely they matched that design.

This also mixes empirical, moral, and policy domains. Historical failure is empirical evidence; the ideal of socialism is a moral or political theory; implementation is a policy and institutional question. One domain cannot automatically erase the others.

Hidden premises:

- Failed cases were not representative enough to count.
- The gap between theory and implementation was caused by external distortion rather than internal incentives.
- A theory should not be judged by predictable implementation failures.
- The speaker has a stable definition of socialism that existed before the historical cases were evaluated.

Falsifiability test:

The claim needs a standard for what would count as a real attempt and what outcome would count against socialism. If no possible state failure can count, the claim is unfalsifiable.

Symmetry check:

The same standard should apply to rival theories. A defender of capitalism, monarchy, democracy, or libertarianism should not be allowed to dismiss every failed implementation as unreal after the fact.

Strongest critique:

This is a no-true-Scotsman pattern unless the speaker supplies criteria in advance. Political theories cannot claim credit for ideal goals while refusing responsibility for recurring institutional failure modes under attempts to implement those goals.

Strongest repair:

A stronger version would say: "Some historical regimes called socialist did not satisfy my definition of socialism because they lacked worker control, democratic accountability, or decentralized ownership. However, those cases may still count as evidence about the risks, incentives, and implementation problems that socialist programs must solve."

Bottom line:

The claim is weak if it uses `real socialism` as an escape hatch. It becomes stronger only if it defines socialism in advance and accepts that failed near-attempts are still relevant evidence.
